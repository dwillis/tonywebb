## NANTWICH.

Rain again on Saturday set hopes and doubts at rest. Silverdale came over and scored 123 runs. The fine weather lasted exactly long enough for the completion of their inning. We had begun to ask ourselves the question whether the wicket and our men were good enough for 121 runs, and our people were shaking their heads, some up and down and amsidoways (signifying Yes and No). Then it rained.

It was five o'clock, and over an hour was spent in story-telling in the pavilion. Seventeen was going to say something about the state of the wicket at Crewe, when it was discovered that "it's gone off," and visitors were turning out their apartments with the umpires. The home side sent out Peerman and Hollowood, and no sooner had they marked the crusae than a drizzle commenced. However, Peerman waited till 2 runs were got and then obliged Lightfoot by clearing the way for Steventon. Two or three overs followed and half a dozen runs were added. Then the rain fell faster and the players returned to aelicter. The spectators were now invisible, and the players were not long in deciding to share the two points, the game being left drawn.

The innings of Silverdale, whose style of play is known to Nantwich from many years' experience, had one very extraordinary feature. T. Clarke and J. Johnson have been companion bowlers since boyhood, and they have never bowled before last Saturday without doing some damage more or less serious. In this match, although they had a good turn each, neither of them took a single wicket. Clarke was beaten by his own side, twice at least. Peerman and Roberts were successful. For 22 Peerman took five wickets, and the other five fell to Roberts for only a few more runs. Nantwich have suffered for want of a change of bowlers, and it is gratifying to see this couple coming out so well.

The innings was also remarkable for had fielding. Certainly the best part of it, F. P. Heynold's 43, was free from fault than anything else. It is only fair to him to say so. His careful butting, something after Barlow's "stone wall" style, was watched by the Kantwich supporters with a little impatience, but it must be admitted that some of our baismon would do much better by following his example.

Nantwich have always had a reputation for good fielding—for their ability to leave, as well as to get, runs. They folded somewhat indifferently against Crowe, and badly against Silverdale. There is a good reason why. It is simply want of practice. When the countrman prophesied that he might become a good Fiddler he was were enough to add that what he wanted was plenty of practice. Good cricketsers, like good Fiddlers, cannot play well without continued practice.

I am glad to say it is not the fault of the Wychers that they have not been up regularly for practice. Depending for their existence more upon work than play, they have been oldred to attend to work first, and that has kept most of them going lately till dick. Tom Bullock was quite right when he said on being asked why he was out of form. "You can't expect a chap to work as long as it is light, going entirely without practice during the week, and also keep up his form in selling or letting on Saturday afternoon, the only time he is free." They have been working harder lately to make up a little for bad times. They will have more leisure now for practice, and we shall soon find the Wycher himself again, like Richard.

Mr. A. N. Hornby had promised to play for Nantwich against Silverdale, but found himself in the morning unable to appear in the field. Mr. Charles Hensall was another absentee from the scene of play, but he found his way to the pavilion. Asked why he had not done it, he white said, he ironically said "No picked." There's some misunderstanding about this, surely.

## WESLEYAN.

On Saturday last we visited the Smallthorne club on their ground at New Ford. The home team won the toss, and put the visitors in on a fairly good wicket. The start was a good one, the first wicket falling at 13, and the second at 20; but after this our wickets fell rapidly, and the whole of us were dismixed for the poor total of 39. Holderott and Gaskoll being the chief contributors to this with 13 and 8 respectively. The home team, unlike us, started belly, but finished up well. Five wickets went down before the required number were knocked off, but the remaining two brought the total score up to 112. Thus the home team won by a very large majority, which might have been considerably less bad our fielding been anything like.

On Saturday we visit Knyporley, when we are hoping to have a very pleasant visit.
