When, in 1888, the subject of our sketch took up his residence in our midst, the term "cricket" was practically a intenomer for the game which was played in what has often been called the "sleepy county." Save for the amateurish expositions with which we were favoured on our village greens, and the but little better exhibitions given by our town clients, "go manly oldo game" had died a natural death. There was then no county oricket club, we could boast few really decent wolders of the willow, and as a county we were far in the rear as regarded England's national game. But better times were coming. Such a beblighted state of affairs was intolerable to the indefatigable and energetic spirit whose life at that time became interwoven with ours, and not many months clapsed or we humble Bedfordshire folk saw that a renaissance was dawning. Space will not permit us to follow minutely our worthy leader in his hereoulan task of roving the all but dead pastime, suffice it to say that, mainly through his persistent efforts and untiring zeal, the game became once more established, the County Cricket Club was formed, and a county team of which from the first we have had cause to be proud, was put into the field.

Hearing these historical facts in mind, we came to the conclusion that a short biographical sketch of “our capitals” would be of the greatest interest to our readers, and, fully intent on “button-holing” the ardent cricketer, we wounded our way the other evening to the scene where he and such like spirits do congregate together. It was after a hard day's work behind the wickets, and when those tyrannical white-coated gentlemen had, to the relief of all parties concerned, reluctantly pocketed the ball, that we spotted our victim stalking in his many, characteristic strides across the sudden turf. We knew the reason. It was our victim's innate modesty which led him to seek to hide the truth from us. But those who know anything about L. O.
