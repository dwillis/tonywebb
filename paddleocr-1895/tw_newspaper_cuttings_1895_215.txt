## S1 LVERDALE. (By the G.C.O.J.M.)

## PLAYING AT KEEPING AJOP.

Did you ever, my dear reader, in your younger days—way as six or seven—play at keeping shop, where imaginary articles, including tea, candles, soft soap, turpentine, and bath-brief: were handed out for as purely imaginary payments, and carried away with a most make-believe air! If so, you can understand Saturday's proceedings, and not without. The team was to play at Cavernwall—noted for its acquire, its ground, and its cricket hospitalities—but everyone entered on the task in a most make-believe fashion. Two players asked to be excused, and one was away for his holidays. They were lucky. The rest, in a dazed sort of manner, was started via Trentham and London, to the ground. I had nearly written desired spot, but no one seemed to desire anything; and there was not even a good cricket tale to keep anyone alive.

The ground duly reached half an hour later, no one thought of beginning, or attempting to begin, and proceeded as leisurely as though it was six in the morning. Another half hour or so passed by, and then someone suggested play, but just on turning up, a photographer turned up, and all seemed to think it was quite the thing to wait and smile while clavesswall eleven, scorer, and ground roller included, tried to make a picture. It was only then that the great one discovered that there was no empire, and that he had to officiate, and did so with many growth.

Mezzak, W. E. Bowers and J. H. Copestako stood in front of the wickets while seven Siderdale players chuckled up the ball in turns, some shorter, some longer. Politeness reigned supreme, and fellow-feeling was wondrous kind. One after the other the folders were presented with the ball to hold, but they begged to be excused, as they did not want to hurt ourself's feelings. Bowling there was none, and with one or two exceptions, I must say no one ever tried. "I think," said one, "that slow bowling is as good as fast on this wicket." "So do I," said the Caverwall captain, as he held his bat for it to hit, "only better." Fifty-three overs were bowled and 102 runs made for one wicket (W. E. Bowesca 37, Copestako, not out, 60), and rain came down. This upset nobody; they still were in the world of make-believe, and even while sitting in the pavilion they passed round an old oil bottle and called it wrinkly. The weather did not clear up, and neither man seemed to wish it would, so at six o'clock the villagers started home. It rained and poured, but no one took any notice. Nobody mentioned crickets and nobody needed, because none had been seen, or attempted; and thus ended one of the most slam and melueholy functions I have ever seen the death of.

The attitude of Bucknall Church renders comment undesirable. An explanation is wanted.

## CONGLETON.

The Forgo Mills and Aisager have fought many battles during their career, and I should say that victories were about equally divided, for it we defeat them at Congleton, they generally manage to take it out of us at Aisager.

Last Saturday saw the first encounter this season, when history again repeated itself, as Alaguer proved the victors on their own meadow. They leave yet to come to Ongleton and, ---but I won't predict. No oriskers need to be told it was a wet day. They were fairly in it. The Fuge was minus four of their regular players, viz., Wild, Buckley, Isaac Minshull, and Johnson, and undertook the journey with nine players, two substitutes being supplied on the ground. Alaguer won the toes, and began their joinings with Pidduck and Dudson, James and Bradley being the howlers. Pidduck was caught by James in Bradley's first over, with 4 runs on only, which had been soared by the other batman. This lot in Capey, and to Dudson and Capey the victory of Alaguer certainly belongs. The partnership realised 33 runs, which was not dissolved until a double change of bowling had been resorted to, Capey being cleverly caught at point by All. Minshull off Lomas. The pair being separated paved the way for further disasters for Alaguer, A. Minshull doing most of the examination, taking seven wickets for 15 runs. Alaguer's innings closed for 53, Dudson being the top scorer with 25. The imings was twice stopped owing to rain. The Forge started with James and Sheard. The first-named was palpsably unwell, and was bowled by Dudson's second ball. A Minshull knocked up 7 in quick time, and was then bowled by Dudson. Sheard went on tatting steadily, and he and Lomas carried the score to 21, the third wicket falling at this total. The visitors, however, could make no headway, and the games being twice suspended, for rain did not improve their chances of success, and the last wicket fell with the score at 39, Alaguer winning by 19 runs.

The Brook Mills betook themselves to Knyperley to play the return match, where the latter cloth took sweet roverge for the defeat they sustained at Congleton a few weeks back. Small scoring was the order on both sides. The Mills's score only amounted to 19, while Knyperley caped this with 25. F. Hardig was principal scorer for Knyperley with 13, and Pointon obtained the same distinction for the Mills with 6. I'ointon also proved a terror with the ball, taking seven wickets for 15 runs.

Although this is James's first season with the Forg, they are putting their whole energies into the affair to give the old pro. a good result, and any of his friends in Staffordshire who are desirous of contributing toward the end will londly communicate with him at his address, Momsley, Cengston, or to Mr Cliffe, Sec. James has a good efficient service both as a player and a catch during his ezisketing career, which has been a long one; and this, coupled with his quiet, unassuming manner, has made him many friends, and is only remains for them to show it by giving him a bumper on the 10th.
