Fenton visited Norton last week for the purpose of annexing points, and were successful; at least, this is what I believe. The scores were—Fenton 94, Norton 56. This ought to entitle Fenton to count two points. Norton will probably agree that it does, but others may think otherwise.

Some people have an idea that I supported the action of Tenton in their match v. Silverhole. How this is arrived at I cannot imagine. I stated sufficiently plain for an avènse reader to understand that I blamed the rules, and that if the rules allowed one team to "best" another nobody could blame the players. This is, to my mind, clear enough. I am of the same opinion now. Those who have been a match at Trent Bridge, when Notishave been playing for a draw will never forget it. How to waste time is shown to perfection. Players having the wickets for a cup of tea and other such little tricks, disgust those who go to see sport. The ways in which a bateman can waste time are numerous, and the same remark applies to a bowler. I hold that the snipires when neutral should be empowered to extend the time for such tested as they consider has been deliberately wasted.

The game of cricket is one of the oldest on record which perhaps accounts for the old-fashioned ways of its legislators. No serious effect is made to advance with events. This is a mistake which is made clearly apparent by the actions of the spectators when a player likes to stoddard goes in to bat. His presence not like magic on a crowd. There is no "fooling around" about him. He goes in to get runs not to waste time, and it must be annoying to a sportsman like him when a bowler uses the power given him by the rules to waste time to the disadvantage of the batter.

The members of the Cricket League may think reform such as I have suggested impracticable, or if possible say they have no power in the matter. That's just where the deadlock is. Nobody appears willing to accept a move. Last year Mr. J. T. Featon suggested that (instead of sure luck deciding which team should have choice of inning) the visitors should have the option of tasting or fielding. There was nothing in the world to prevent this being done in League matches, but the committee wouldn't have it. The majority of the committee preferred to trust to luck to assist in winning matches. Winning the loss has more to do with a Saturday afternoon match than most people imagine, and if a captain is fortunate enough to guess the upper side of a penny in both matches he stands a good chance of securing four points, or at the least the opportunity of making his opponents take the field the best portion of the time.

Luck is an element which ought not to be counted upon as an assistance to secure victory, and although not a thought-reader, I venture to think that 99 out of every 100 spectators will agree with what I say. On a bitterly cold day six or eight thousand people will put in an appearance at Sake to see a good football match, because they know that they will get a full hour and a half's sport. Waste of time is not permitted there, and luck is reduced to a minimum. The clavest team as a rule was the game, and that is a lot more than can be said about present-day cricket.
