KENTISH EXPRESS: FRIDAY 15 SEPTEMBER 1939-2.

KENT'S BETTER SEASON

BOWLING MORE CONSISTENT THAN BATTING

By L.B.W.

Through the cancelling of the Kent and Middlesex match, which was to have been played at Lord's, the Kent season came to an end with the brilliant victory over Lancashire at Dover on August 29.

Here is a list of matches played and the results, which left Kent fifth in the championship table. Last year we were ninth.

When young Peter Foster came into the team a good many judges of the game said he was not quite up to first-class form and in the first three matches he lived up to this reputation. In the middle of the season, however, he greatly improved and his 107 against Leicester was a delightful innings. You could not at present call his defence very sound, but he possesses some charming strokes.

Todd has maintained his position as the most valuable all-round man in the eleven. If anything he has been over-bowled and it must be remembered that he is the first to attack against the host batsmen. His batting has naturally suffered, but at times he has heroically stopped a collapse and three centuries. A fine record.

The rest of our batsmen have been disappointing. Wright and Harding have not come up to expectations in that part of the game.

Coming to the bowling, we find Wright far ahead of the others. At times he simply slaughtered batsmen. He has increased his pace this year and the quickness with which his break comes in from the leg has upset the best batsmen.

Watt has done well at times with his shorter run and slower deliveries. Harding has improved upon last year and next year, if there is cricket, he will be more deadly. Lewis greatly improved and when Wright was away in the Tests did wonderfully well. It is worth his while to practise batting. The other bowler to mention is J. W. Martin, the Catford amateur fast bowler. Given more practice in county cricket he should improve.

Thus we come to an end of the cricket season of 1939—a tragic end. Let us hope and pray that county cricket will be played again next season.

Generally speaking I can only repeat what I have continually said during the season—that as a whole our bowlers have been more consistent than our batsmen. No really big scores have been amassed against us. In Levett we have had a first-class wicket-keeper and have suffered in no way from the absence of Ames behind the stumps.

In regard to the leading batsmen, we have never known what each would do. One of them might play in a couple of matches and never get into double figures, then he would burst forth with a century, or even two. Absolute inconsistency. But it shows the fascinating uncertainty of cricket.

Now let us look at the individual play. Fagg is one of those extraordinary batsmen who can play with the utmost caution for hours and then suddenly start to drive and cut brilliantly, while he is absolutely deadly on the leg side. He hit up five centuries, but many a time had a bad spell. In fact, in his first nine innings he totalled 98 runs. Then he played one of his centuries, and this was his form throughout the season—inconsistency. But what a fine bat he is when set!

Ames started in a similar way to Fagg. In his first thirteen innings this season he totalled only 245 runs. Then against Glamorgan he made 177. He had other spells of low scoring, followed by a century, but looking at his batting for the whole season, he must be placed as the most reliable of our eleven. No-one who saw it will ever forget his wonderful performance at the Oval when he made 136 in 68 minutes and won the £100 annually given to the batsman who makes a century in the quickest time.

Next in the averages is B. H. Valentine, and here again we had splashes of brilliancy interspersed with failures. He is one of those batsmen feared by the opposite side. When once he gets going all bowlers seem powerless, but his great failure is a desire to start scoring a little too quickly, and for that reason he has lost his wicket many a time in the first few overs.

The Kent skipper, P. G. H. Chalk, has the courage to take an independent course quite contrary to the policy of most captains. He has been criticised for putting in Yorkshire at Dover and also neglecting to make Sussex follow-on at Hastings. In one case he was successful, but in the other, at Dover, it was a definite failure.
