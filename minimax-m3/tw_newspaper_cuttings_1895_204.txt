Saturday last would not have been considered, for cricketers, a meteorological success. All the teams, however, met to fulfil their engagements, but unlike the matches that were played on the 25th ult.—when all the teams were secured by the weather in the same fashion—some of the matches last week were drawn, and some were completed.

The principal match of the day was probably the one played by Burslem against the County at Stoke. It was rendered all the more important by the fact that the County put in the field the strongest team it has ever played. In this match Mr. A. H. Heath figure for the first time in League cricket. Notwithstanding a good commencement by the County, when the scoring board showed two wickets down for 43 runs, the whole eleven could only muster 65 runs. This was the result of some splendid bowling by Hulme, who had the wonderful average of nine wickets for 25 runs. Burslem made but short work of this score, and at the completion of the get had scored 68 runs for five wickets—Brown, hit out, 31, and Brownfield 12, being the principal scorers. Burslem tried by five wickets and 3 runs.

The five-holders had a week-end at Leek, where they opened the new Mountaint Cricket Ground. This match was not completed, several adjournments for rain interfering with the time. Leek appeared to best advantage, and helped by a score of 65 by their professional Kirkby, and by Messrs. Wright and Brunt, who also scored usefully, the side manipulated 111 when it declared its innings closed with the loss of nine wickets. To this Grove made rather a feeble response, and uncertain as the game is known to be, it can be safely assumed that Grove is satisfied with the one point it has obtained. At 32 for four wickets by Crewe the game was abandoned.

Norton and Fenton saw the completion of their match. Leek mustered up by a hit of good scoring at the completion of the innings, made 94 runs, a score which appeared at one time to be very improbable. Birks, Rhodes, J. Turner, and Doe were the chief batsmen. Norton could only respond with 56 runs, and thus lost their fourth League match.

The scoring match of the day was played at Tunstall, where Porthill seems to have done with the home team pretty well what it liked. At 182 runs for three wickets, the highest score for three wickets that has ever been recorded in the League, Porthill declared its innings closed, and Tunstall responded with but 102 runs, and thus were very easily beaten. In this match Porthill are to be congratulated upon the scores made by Messrs. Bennett and King, their professionals, who made 52 not out and 54 respectively, and H. F. Wood, 34, with Mr. Dean, who made 21. Tunstall's performance would have been mediocre in the extreme had it not have been for the last wicket performances by Messrs. Buckley (23) and Gater (25).

Silverdale journeyed to Nantwich, where another drawn game was played. Silverdale making the useful score of 123 when rain stopped play. Silverdale's record was strongly assisted by its left-handed batsmen—Messrs. Reynolds, Burrows, and Johnson, who made 45, 22, and 12 respectively. On Whit-Monday Silverdale met Burslem before a very large assembly of spectators, and although strongly pronounced by the absence of Walhgate and Lightfoot, they inflicted a severe defeat upon the brother town, scoring 58 for two wickets of against 54 by the Burslem team. This match was a most sensational one, 30 runs being scored by Burslem without the loss of a wicket, when almost with consecutive balls Hulme, Garner, Brownfield, and Brown were all taken, and the completion of the innings was simply a procession to the pavilion. Silverdale's score was almost entirely made by Messrs. Calfkin and Reynolds, who under the circumstances batted well and insured for their side a complete victory.

The principal features of these matches can be briefly noted. To make nearly 300 runs in an afternoon that is somewhat interfered with by rain, prove a has been previously stated, that Tunstall had the easiest scoring ground in the League. The in and out play of Burslem in the matches played on Saturday and Monday is another item that must be noted by those who follow League cricket. The position of the League table is becoming interesting, very little difference being noticeable between the position occupied by the clubs who hold the medium positions. None of the first three clubs have met each other at present, and there can be no doubt that the matches between them will be most eagerly contested. To-day all the teams are occupied again. Burslem v. Norton, County v. Leek, Torehill v. Fenton, Silverdale v. Crewe, and Tunstall v. Nantwich.

NANTWICH.

Rain again on Saturday set hopes and doubts at rest. Silverdale came over and scored 123 runs. The fine weather lasted exactly long enough for the completion of their innings. We had begun to ask ourselves the question whether the wicket and our men were good enough for 124 runs, and our people were shaking their heads some up and down and anxiously—wondering. This was 14. This patience was greatly tantalising! It is something to draw the game, and over an hour was spent in story-telling in the pavilion. Stevenson was going to say something about the state of the wicket at

Crewe, when it was discovered that "it's gone off," and visitors were turning out of their apartments with the umpires. The home side sent out Peerman and Hollinwood, and no sooner had they marked the crease than a drizzle commenced. However, Peerman waited till 2 runs were got and then signalled Lightfoot by clearing the way for Stevenson. Two or three overs followed and half a dozen runs were added. Then the rain fell faster and the players returned to shelter. The spectators were now invisible, and the players were not long in deciding to share the two points, the game being left drawn.

The innings of Silverdale, whose style of play is known to Nantwich from many years' experience, had one very extraordinary feature. T. Clarke and J. Johnson have been companion bowlers since boyhood, and they have never bowled before last Saturday without doing some damage more or less serious. In this match, although they had a good turn each, neither of them took a single wicket. Clarke was beaten by his own side, twice at least. Peerman and Roberts were successful. For 22 Peerman took five wickets, and the other five fell to Roberts for only a few more runs. Nantwich have suffered for want of a change of bowlers, and it is gratifying to see this couple coming out so well.

The innings was also remarkable for bad fielding. Certainly the best part of it, V. P. Reynolds's 43, came from faults fielding else, It is only fair to him to say so. His careful batting, something after Barlow's "stone-wall" style, was watched by the Nantwich supporters with a little impatience, but it must be admitted that some of our batsmen would do much better by following his example.

Nantwich have always had a reputation for good fielding—for their ability to save, as well as to get, runs. They fielded somewhat indifferently against Crewe, and badly against Silverdale. There is a good reason why. It is simply want of practice. When the countrymen complained that he might be become a good fielder he was once enough as to be thus what he wanted was plenty of practice. Good cricketers, like good fielders, cannot play well without continued practice.

I am glad to say it is not the fault of the Wrekin that they have not been up equally for practice. Depaeding for their existence more upon work than play, they have been obliged to attend to work first, and that has kept most of them going lately till dusk. Tom Bullock was quite right when he said on being asked why he was out of form. "You can't expect a chap to work as long as it light, and conversly you can't practise during the week, and also keep up his form in fielding or batting on Saturday afternoon, the only time he is free." They have been working harder lately to make up a little for bad times. They will have more leisure now for practice, and we shall soon find the Wrekin himself again, like Richard.

Mr. A. N. Hornby had promised to play for Nantwich against Silverdale, but found himself in the morning unable to appear in the field. Mr. Charles Hassall was another absentee from the scene of play, but he found his way to the pavilion. Asked why he did not donned his white kit, he laconically said "Not picked." There's some misunderstanding about this, surely.

WESLEYAN.

On Saturday last we visited the Smallthorne club on their ground at New Ford. The home team won the toss, and put the visitors in on a fairly good wicket. The start was a good one, the first wicket falling at 13, and the second at 26; but after this our wickets fell rapidly, and the whole of us were dismissed for the poor total of 39. Holdcroft and Gaskell being the chief contributors to this with 13 and 9 respectively. The home team, unlike us, started badly, but finished up well. Five wickets went down before the required number were knocked off, but the remaining five brought the total score up to 112. Thus the home team won by a very large majority, which might have been considerably less had our fielding been anything like.

On Saturday we visit Knypersley, when we are hoping to have a very pleasant out.
