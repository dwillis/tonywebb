LEIGHTON v. OXFORD HARLEQUINS.
The annual match between these teams took place
on the Leighton ground on Monday and Tuesday. On
the first day the game was witnessed by upwards of 1,000
spectators. This number was far in excess of the
attendance at last years match, and as practically the
same county players, with the exception of Ranjitsinhji,
were in the field on the last occasion, it may justly be
assumed that the increased attendance was due to the
inclusion in the Leighton team of this popular young
Indian player, who by his brilliant and consistent play,
both with the bat and in the field, has gained for him-
self the soubriquet of the "Indian Grace of Cricket." Mr.
Laverton had strengthened his team, not only by the
services of Ranjitsinhji, but by Brain, the Sussex county
bowler, and Trask, one of the Somerset reserves, while
Fry, the Oxford triple blue, who last year played for
Leighton, transferred his allegiance to the visitors. The
Harlequins brought a very strong team, including five
of the Somerset county team, no doubt resolving to
avenge the defeat they suffered at the hands of Leighton
last year. Glorious cricketing weather prevailed
throughout the day, and nothing further could be wished
for in this respect. Newman had prepared a splendid
pitch, and with a hard and true wicket it was inevitable
that high scoring would be witnessed. The spin of the
coin was in Mr. Laverton's favour, and, electing to send the
visitors to the field, F. W. Stancomb and W. Trask went to
the wicket just on the stroke of 12, Fry and Cassan taking
the ball. Stancomb played a very steady and valuable
game, defending his wickets for nearly two hours for
24 runs, being ultimately caught at short leg
by L. Palairot. Ranjitsinhji took first wicket down. His
play was undoubtedly the feature of the game. In an
exceedingly graceful and faultless style, and with some
especially pretty hits to leg, he won the admiration of
every cricketer on the ground, and it was very hard lines
that after putting together 39, he was stumped by Brain
from Roe, the ball bounding on to the wicket from the
wicket-keeper's guards. Brain contributed a useful 35,
his innings being a rather lucky one, as he was several
times missed in the field. Pontifex had the honour of
compiling the highest score. He played dashing cricket
for his innings of 61, his fine cuts being especially
admired. His score included a six (which included three
overthrows), five fours, two threes, and two twos. The
only other men to reach double figures were Shorland
and Stancomb, the former scoring 15 by some hard
hitting, and the latter making some pretty cuts for four
and threes in his score of 10. The innings closed soon
after four for 339. The fielding on the whole was good,
but there was an extraordinary large number of extras,
owing to Brain, the wicket-keeper, standing in close to
the fast bowling. Cassan's tricky bowling was by far the
most effective, and he succeeded in taking six wickets
for 70 runs. The visitors commenced their innings at
4.20. H. C. N. Palairot and Hill went first to the wickets,
while Trask and Atfield were put on to bowl. Runs
came very quickly, but Palairot was soon caught in the
slips by Shorland, the telegraph board showing 30-1-14.
His score included some pretty cuts. Hill was joined
by Fry, and these two quickly made matters lively, and
Laverton found it advisable to augment the long field.
Some dashing cricket was witnessed, and bowlers were
frequently changed, but without the desired effect. Hill
was badly missed at the wicket by F. W. Stancomb when
he had scored 21, and Fry, too, was somewhat favoured
before he had reached 40. A. G. Stancomb brought off a
beautiful catch in the long field. Hill passed his century
by a fine cut to long leg having been batting just over an
hour. Ranjitsinhji, however, ultimately succeeded in
doing the needful. Hill, tempted by a simple straight
ball, jumped out, and attempted to pull to leg, with the
result that he missed and his stumps were scattered.
Throughout his innings he played in dashing style, his
score of 115 including one six, nine fours, nine threes, 13
twos. Fry was now joined by Palairot, and these two
entering partnership at 6.15 carried the score to 199
before play was suspended for the day.
Ideal weather also prevailed on Tuesday, and there
was again a large attendance of spectators, who through-
out showed great interest in the game, cheering im-
partially anything extraordinary smart on the part of
the players, irrespective of which side they belonged.
The wicket was even faster than on the previous day,
and consequently some grand scoring was witnessed.
Trask and Atfield were again put on to bowl. Palairot
took the first ball, with the result that the double
century was reached. Newman, one of the umpires, had
to decide a knotty question early in the play. Fry, in
defending his wickets, hit the ball twice, the second time
playing it in the wicket-keeper's hands. The bowler
appealed, and the umpire decided in favour of the bats-
man, but opinion was freely expressed that the decision
was an erroneous one. Under-hand lobs somewhat
puzzled the batsmen, but they soon got used to it, and it
was evident they were settled for a long partnership.
Bowlers were changed in quick succession, but still
the score rapidly increased, and after a length
Fry's wicket fell to Atfield the total had been
ree 3-369-3-163. Fry at times was in brilliant form,
and some very smart hits to boundaries were witnessed.
His score was composed of one six, 19 fours, six threes,
and 16 twos. Roe filled the vacancy, and 400 was reached
just before one o'clock. Roe's stay at the wickets was
very short, for having scored 15, including a tremendous
hit into the next field, he was caught at long-off by
Laverton, who brought off a really grand catch. Brain
came in next, and soon after Palairot was caught at mid-
off by Ranjitsinhji, the ball spinning in the air off the
edge of the bat. Dunlop was the new comer, but Ranji-
sinhji at mid-off was again successful, this time Dunlop
being the victim from a ball by Atfield. Brain, how-
ever, failed to take the lesson to heart, and Atfield,
sending down a similar ball, he hit out in precisely the
same manner as his late partner, the result being that
Ranjitsinhji, ever on the alert, for the third time in
succession brought off a very difficult catch, taking the
ball high up with his left hand—a performance which
provoked a round of applause. 446 was recorded
at lunch time for the loss of eight wickets, and L. C. N.,

thinking there was a possibility of dismissing the op-
posing team for a smaller sum, declared the innings
closed. Leighton commenced their second attempt just
after two o'clock, Stancomb and Trask taking the bat
against the bowling of Fry and Cassan. The former,
having smartly scored 21, was dismissed by a
splendid catch by Hill at long leg. Ranjitsinhji again
took first wicket down, his appearance from the pavilion
being greeted with a hearty round of applause, and it is
very evident that he had won the good opinions of the
people of this part of the world. For two hours and
forty minutes he played a faultless game. Commencing
very cautiously he soon warmed to his work, and
boundary followed boundary in quick succession. It
was seldom that he made a faulty stroke; there were
absolutely no lofty hits, but good crisp strokes all along
the carpet. The frequent changes in the bowling did
not trouble him, and Cassan's tricky bowling and Fry's
tremendous fast ones did not discomfort him in the least.
He was eventually smartly caught by Brain at point, the
ball going and covering some to boundary over the
fieldsman's head. It is almost needless to add that he
received an ovation on returning to the pavilion. His
score was composed of 21 fours, two threes, and 14 twos.
Rose composed [unclear] was shown by remainder of
the team, but after Ranjitsinhji's disappearance from the
scene of action all interest was gone in the game. Two
total reached 331, so that in the two innings Leighton
made 570 for 18 wickets, while their opponents compiled
446 for eight wickets. It will be seen from these figures
that no less than 1,016 runs were scored during the two
days, and in this respect a new record was made, the
highest number of runs made previously in any one
match being just over 900. Although the visitors had
undoubtedly the best of the match the home team
on the whole played a sterling game, their weakest point
being certainly in the fielding, and in this respect they
were decidedly inferior to the visitors. If the chances
given by Hill, Fry, and Palairot before they had scored
very high had been successfully brought off the match
would have assumed a totally different complexion, for
the visitors' total, shorn of the three centuries, would
have looked insignificant against that of their opponents.
It will be noticed with gratification that Atfield, the
popular Leighton pro., secured the best bowling average,
notwithstanding the hard luck he had in the field.

TETBURY v. G. SAINSBURY'S XI.—This match was
played on Westonbirt cricket ground on Saturday, and
ended in a win for G. Sainsbury's XI. by 53 runs on the
first innings. Great credit is due to the brothers May
and B. Sharp, of Malmesbury, who played brilliantly for
the winners.
