# WESTERN DAILY PRESS: FRIDAY 16 JUNE 1939

# SPORTING MEMORIES Bristol Cricket of Long Ago CLIFTON RUGBY HISTORY

## (By A.G.P.)

THE Gloucestershire team are having a wonderful season and the strange part of the cheerful aspect of things is that they have got to the second position in the championship table without having once had the supposed advantage of batting first.

And the most encouraging feature of the season's results is that the successes have been won very largely by the side generally rather than the dominating form of one or two men

To-morrow the XI. villi be at Worthing Sussex are due for a come back. Will this be the occasion? For a good many years past Sussex has more often than not been well on top in their games with Gloucestershire.

Mr Frank Pratten has been good enough to let me have a look at the Clifton Rugby Club's minute books for their first and second seasons. 1872-3 and 1873-4

The first secretary was W. J. G. Levell and presumably the minutes are recorded in his handwriting. And beautiful writing it is—almost copper-plate.

The original list of members and their addresses are set out in full. They include many familiar names—Wilberforce, Tribe, and Frank Wills, who later became Sir Frank, being among them No Bush's ajar. J. A. and R. E. were at that time playing for Bedminster and joined Clifton when the first club in Bristol broke up.

There is, by the by, a newspaper report of a match between Clifton and Bedminster stuck in the book. It took place at Bedminster on November 16, 1872, and was won by the home team, with the core:—Bedminster, 9 rouges, 6 touchdowns; Clifton, nil.

The report states that the touchdowns were secured for Bedminster by S. W. L. Llewellyn, G. H. Pepler and W. H. Brown. The last named was for many years chairman of the Bristol Cricket Association and father of W. S. A. Brown's fine player for Gloucestershire under Dr. W. G. Grace and Gilbert Jessop.

Bedminster's success is somewhat discounted by the fact that all their 20 players turned up but bad weather kept seven of the Clifton men away. A postscript adds that the Clifton secretary could not get the names of the Bedminster 20. What a pity!

In a return game that season Clifton won by 3 rouges to nil. A third game gave Clifton the victory by 2 tries, 3 rouges and 3 touches in goal.

Further reference to these old books will be made.

## JAMES CRANSTON

A reader of this column sends a cutting from the 'Birmingham Post' which appeared under the heading "Fifty Years Ago." It has reference to a fine old Gloucestershire cricketer—James Cranston. The paragraph stated:—“If any of our readers have aught against the Warwickshire Cricket Club Committee, and would wreak a deadly revenge, let him murmur in their ears the one word 'Cranston.' The result is instantaneous paralysis. Three years ago Mr James Cranston dropped the residential qualification under which he had for several years played for Gloucestershire. He had been distinctly the best left-handed businessman of the day. He had a birth qualification for Warwickshire

The committee played him three times, twice against Surrey and once against the Australians, and then dropped him almost contemptuously. This year he has again a qualification for Gloucestershire, and has been eagerly welcomed to the team. He has come off in every match, and his batting, so far, has been quite one of the features of the season.

"Jimmy" Cranston will be well remembered by old cricketers in this district. He was in great form: in the season to which the Birmingham Post" referred.

Against Survey at the Oval, he scored 111 and at Brighton, against Sussex, 139. Just afterwards he played for Westbury against the United Press on a pitch at the County Ground with a ridiculously short boundary. Four balls were lost in one over bowled to him by the writer of these notes!

## S. A. P. KITCAT

Another good old Gloucestershire cricketer is brought to mind by an inquiry as to when S. A. P. Kiteat first played for the county and in what year did he take part in a grand partnership with Dr. W. G. Grace.

The reply is 1892 as the date of his first appearance for the county, and 1896 when he made 77 not out against Sussex at the County Ground.

That innings will surely remain in the memory of Mr Kitcat, as it does in the writer's and many others. It was the April Bank Holiday game, and glorious weather. Dr. W. G. Grace won the toss and, batting all day, was 193 not out at the close

The next day five wickets went down for very few runs and when Kitcat came in there was only one man—Roberts—to follow. But the young amateur played a brilliant innings of 77 not out while his famous captain raised his score to 301 and the total was advanced from 355 to 550.

That match, which Gloucestershire won by an innings and 123, and in which the great Prince Ranjitsinghi scored 38 and 54 for Sussex, will always remain in my mind as the best holiday match at the County Ground during the past 50 years.

Mr Kitcat had another memorable experience with Dr. W. G. Grace. He was in the team during that wonderful May, 1895, when the "Old Man" made cricket history by scoring 1,000 runs before the month was over. At Gravesend, when Dr. Grace was in the field the whole of the three days, his best partner in making 257 runs was S. A. P. Kitcat, who played a fine innings of 52, when other members of the team, except Jack Painter, found runs hard to get.

Mr Kitcat played his last game for Gloucestershire in 1899. He is remembered as a most attractive batsman—not a stylist but possessed of a sound defence and a hard hitter when the right ball came along.

## FOUR GRACE BROTHERS

When the Bath and West Show was on the Downs in 1864, our proprietors issued a supplement to the ordinary 'Daily Press' for June 15.

In one corner of this old newspaper I came across the scores of a cricket match in which four of the famous Grace brothers, Henry, Alfred, W. G., and Fred, took part.

The game was Hanham v. Stapleton, and it was played at Pudway Hill, Rodway.

William Gilbert, who became the champion of all cricketers, and Henry played for Hanham, and Alfred and Fred (then 14) for Stapleton.

Young Fred had taken four of the five wickets, including those of W. G. and Henry, when rain stopped play.

Fred Grace was the best bowler in the famous family. When only nine years old he took 13 wickets in a match—all grown men.
