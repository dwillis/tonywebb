Reading 130-Year-Old Cricket Statistics with AI

An experiment with the Tony Webb Minor Counties Collection, 1895

The Collection

Cricket has always been a sport of records. The Tony Webb Minor Counties Collection holds hundreds of newspaper cuttings from the 1890s — brittle pages, faded ink, figures meticulously recorded by correspondents long before the idea of a digital archive could have existed. These cuttings document matches, season averages, and club news from the corners of English cricket that the county game rarely covered: village clubs, school XIs, works teams, and the competitive leagues of the industrial north and midlands.

The collection has been scanned and made available online by the Association of Cricket Statisticians and Historians. The page below — page 58 — is representative of what much of the collection looks like: dense columns of figures, several clubs' averages running in sequence, abbreviations that any Victorian cricket reader would recognise at a glance but that are invisible to a computer searching for patterns.

The question this experiment asks is simple: can a large language model read these pages accurately and return reliable, structured data that researchers and enthusiasts could actually use?

The Source: Page 58

This page carries end-of-season batting and bowling averages for five clubs from the Liverpool district: Liverpool CC, Northern, Birkenhead Victoria, Prescot, and Formby. Each club's section opens with a one-line season summary (matches played, won, drawn, lost; aggregate runs for and against), then lists every player who batted or bowled regularly, with their accumulated figures for the year. The columns are clearly labelled, but the text is compressed and the layout assumes a reader who already knows what they are looking at.

How It Works

The extraction runs in two stages, both relying on AI. Neither requires anyone to write specific rules for 1895 cricket averages tables. Instead, each model is given instructions in plain language and left to work out the details.

1
Transcription. A vision model reads each scanned page image and produces plain text. This goes beyond simple OCR: the model understands enough context to distinguish a player's initials from a bowling average, and to recognise that a dagger or asterisk means "not out" even when the symbol is faint or slightly misaligned. The plain text it produces is the raw material for step two.
2
Extraction. A second model receives the plain text alongside a set of instructions. It is asked to find any end-of-season averages tables, identify which club each table belongs to, and return everything as structured data — team records, batting figures, bowling figures — in a format that can be stored and searched. The result is a JSON object, a few pages' worth of cricket history now fully machine-readable.

What makes the second step interesting is that the instructions do not need to anticipate every quirk of Victorian cricket writing. The model already understands that "not out" matters for batting averages, that bowling analyses use overs and maidens, and that a season summary line precedes the player tables. What the instructions do specify are the things that would trip up any reader new to this particular collection — how to format names, what counts as a separate entry, when to leave a figure blank rather than guess. That combination of general cricket knowledge and specific guidance is what makes the output usable.

There are errors — the occasional misread figure, a name that defeats even careful instructions. But across a collection of this size, running hundreds of pages automatically and correcting the outliers is far faster than transcribing every table by hand. The potential is not to replace human expertise, but to make it possible to apply that expertise at scale.

The model returns a single JSON object. A short script checks that it is valid, normalises player names and club names to a consistent style, and writes the results to a running file. The whole process for a page like this — five clubs, over a hundred players — takes a few seconds.
